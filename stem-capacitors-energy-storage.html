01 · WHAT A CAPACITOR DOES

Store charge, then release energy

A capacitor consists of two conductive plates separated by an insulating material called a dielectric. When a voltage is applied, charge accumulates on the plates and energy is stored in the electric field.

FOUNDATION

How it stores energy

Charge on one plate is balanced by opposite charge on the other. The dielectric prevents direct conduction between the plates under normal operating conditions.

Capacitance and units

Q = C × V

Capacitance describes how much charge is stored per volt. The SI unit is the farad (F). Practical components are commonly labelled in microfarads (µF), nanofarads (nF) or picofarads (pF).

  • 1 µF = 1,000 nF
  • 1 nF = 1,000 pF
  • 1 F = 1,000,000 µF

For an ideal capacitor, charge is proportional to voltage. The actual component also has tolerance, leakage, equivalent series resistance (ESR) and voltage/temperature limits.

Important distinction: a capacitor is not a source of unlimited energy. It stores a finite amount of energy determined by its capacitance and voltage. Its voltage can remain present after power is disconnected.

02 · TYPES AND POLARITY

Different capacitors suit different jobs

Learn the common families and check the markings before connecting a component.

104

Ceramic

Common for small capacitance values, high-frequency bypassing and decoupling. Most common ceramic parts are non-polarised. Their capacitance can vary with dielectric type, voltage and temperature.

MKP

Film

Often used for timing, signal coupling, filtering and pulse applications. Film capacitors are generally non-polarised, but voltage, frequency and pulse ratings still matter.

+−

Aluminium electrolytic

Offers relatively high capacitance in a compact package. Most are polarised. The stripe commonly identifies the negative terminal; the longer lead on a new radial part is often positive, but verify the actual marking.

+

Tantalum

Compact polarised capacitors used in some low-voltage electronics. Polarity markings vary by manufacturer and package. They can fail dangerously if incorrectly connected or overstressed.

Never guess capacitor polarity from appearance alone. Check the printed marking and datasheet. Do not reuse capacitors that are bulging, leaking, cracked, corroded or otherwise damaged.

03 · CIRCUIT SYMBOLS

Read capacitor symbols and connections

The two-plate symbol represents a non-polarised capacitor. A curved plate or a plus sign is commonly used to indicate a polarised capacitor.

Non-polarised capacitor

Either terminal can normally connect to either side of the circuit, subject to its ratings.

Polarised capacitor

The positive terminal must be kept at a higher potential than the negative terminal within the part's specified operating conditions.

Change the resistance, capacitance and elapsed time. The ideal RC model shows how the capacitor voltage changes over time.

Ideal behavioural model

At one time constant, a charging capacitor reaches about 63.2% of its final voltage.

Model assumptions: ideal capacitor, constant resistor, zero leakage, no ESR and an ideal voltage source. Real components and circuits deviate from this curve.

What is one time constant? τ = R × C. During charging, the capacitor reaches about 63.2% of its final voltage after one τ. During discharging, it falls to about 36.8% of its starting voltage. After five τ, the change is usually treated as practically complete for many basic calculations, not mathematically absolute.

05 · CAPACITORS IN COMBINATIONS

Calculate series and parallel capacitance

Capacitor combinations behave differently from resistor combinations.

Equivalent capacitance20 µF

Parallel connection

Ceq = C1 + C2 + …

Capacitances add. The voltage across each ideal parallel capacitor is the same. The combined capacitance is greater than any individual capacitance.

Series connection

1/Ceq = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + …

For two capacitors, Ceq = (C1 × C2) ÷ (C1 + C2). The same charge magnitude appears on each ideal series capacitor, while the applied voltage is shared.

Do not assume series-connected polarised capacitors will share voltage equally in real circuits. Practical designs may need balancing components and datasheet-based checks.

06 · WHERE CAPACITORS ARE USED

Timing, filtering and power conditioning

Capacitors are used in many circuit functions, but the right type and value depend on the application.

Timing and delay

An RC network creates a predictable charge or discharge curve. It can form timing intervals, reset delays and simple oscillator networks.

Filtering and smoothing

Capacitors can reduce voltage ripple and shunt unwanted signal variations, depending on circuit impedance and frequency.

Decoupling and bypass

A capacitor placed close to an IC supply pin helps provide transient current and reduce local supply disturbances. Layout and capacitor characteristics matter.

AC coupling

A series capacitor can pass changing signal components while blocking a steady DC level, when used with a suitable input/output impedance.

Capacitor after a rectifier

A reservoir capacitor charges near the rectified waveform peaks and supplies the load between peaks, reducing ripple. The ripple depends on load current, capacitance, rectifier frequency and other circuit conditions. This conceptual diagram is not a mains power-supply construction guide.

07 · SELECTING A CAPACITOR

More than just the capacitance value

Check the full operating conditions and datasheet before selecting a part.

CapacitanceF, µF, nF or pF

Nominal value and tolerance determine the expected range.

Voltage ratingWorking voltage

Do not exceed the rated voltage. Allow suitable design margin.

PolarityPolarised or non-polarised

Observe positive and negative markings where applicable.

ESREquivalent series resistance

Affects ripple heating, losses and transient response.

Ripple currentAC current rating

Important in power filtering; excessive ripple current causes heating.

Temperature and lifeEnvironmental limits

Temperature affects performance and lifetime, especially for electrolytics.

08 · WORKBENCH PRACTICE

Inspect and measure a low-voltage capacitor

Use a known, low-voltage capacitor in a supervised extra-low-voltage circuit.

LOW-VOLTAGE ACTIVITY

Before measuring

  1. Identify the capacitor type, capacitance, voltage rating and polarity markings.
  2. Inspect the body and leads. Do not use a bulging, leaking, cracked or damaged component.
  3. Disconnect the circuit from its supply.
  4. If the capacitor may be charged, discharge it through a suitable resistor chosen for the capacitor and voltage. Do not short the terminals with a screwdriver or wire.
  5. Verify with a multimeter in DC-voltage mode that the voltage has fallen to a safe, near-zero level before handling or changing connections.
  6. Use the meter's capacitance function only as described in its manual, with the capacitor isolated and discharged.

Simple RC observation

  1. Use a small known capacitor and a current-limiting resistor with an isolated, low-voltage DC source.
  2. Observe capacitor voltage with a multimeter while it charges through the resistor.
  3. Disconnect the supply and allow it to discharge through the designed resistor path.
  4. Compare the observed timing with τ = RC. Record differences and possible causes such as tolerance, meter loading, leakage and component ESR.

Do not experiment with unknown capacitors, mains equipment, microwave/flash circuits, inverter DC links or other high-energy assemblies. Some can retain dangerous voltage long after disconnection.

09 · COMMON MISTAKES

What can go wrong?

Reversing a polarised capacitor

Incorrect polarity can cause heating, leakage, venting or failure. Verify the marking and circuit polarity before applying power.

Ignoring the voltage rating

A capacitor's rated voltage is not a target operating voltage to exceed. Choose a suitable part with design margin.

Assuming the capacitor is discharged

Stored charge can remain after the supply is removed. Use an appropriate resistor and verify with a meter.

Choosing capacitance alone

ESR, ripple current, temperature, tolerance, lifetime and frequency behaviour may be just as important.
