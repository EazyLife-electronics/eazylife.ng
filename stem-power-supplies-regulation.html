01 · WHY POWER SUPPLIES MATTER

A circuit needs the right kind of power

A power supply converts energy from a source into electrical power with voltage, current capability, polarity, noise and stability suitable for a load. A circuit marked “5 V” does not mean every 5 V source is safe: the source must also tolerate the load, start-up current, transients and wiring losses.

From source to regulated DC

This is a functional block diagram. Some modern supplies use switching conversion rather than a linear regulator, and many DC-input supplies do not need an AC rectifier stage.

Four questions before choosing a supply: What voltage does the load require? How much continuous and peak current does it draw? How clean and stable must the rail be? What happens during fault, overload, start-up and loss of power?

02 · RECTIFICATION AND SMOOTHING

Turn low-voltage AC into smoother DC

A bridge rectifier uses four diodes so both halves of an AC waveform produce current in the same direction through the load. A reservoir capacitor charges near waveform peaks and supplies the load between peaks. The output is pulsating DC with ripple, not perfectly flat DC.

Full-wave bridge rectifier

Conceptual bridge topology. The positive and negative DC rails are across the bridge output; the reservoir capacitor and load connect in parallel across those rails. For real wiring, follow a bridge module's printed AC and +/− markings or a verified diode schematic, not the artistic placement alone.

Safety boundary: all practical activities in this lesson use a certified, isolated, extra-low-voltage AC adapter or a current-limited DC bench supply. Do not connect a breadboard, exposed bridge, capacitor or regulator circuit directly to household mains. Do not build an exposed mains transformer or line-powered supply here.

What the capacitor does

The reservoir capacitor charges near the peaks of the rectified waveform and discharges into the load as the rectified voltage falls. Larger capacitance generally reduces ripple for the same load current and ripple frequency, but increases inrush current and diode/transformer stress.

C ≈ ILOAD / (fRIPPLE × VRIPPLE)

For full-wave rectification, ripple frequency is twice the AC frequency: 100 Hz from 50 Hz AC, or 120 Hz from 60 Hz AC.

Ripple is not the same as regulation

Ripple is the periodic variation remaining on a DC rail. Load regulation describes how much the average output changes when the load current changes. Line regulation describes how much output changes when the input voltage changes.

A regulator can reduce ripple and variation, but it cannot correct an input that falls below its required operating headroom.

03 · LINEAR REGULATORS

Simple regulation with a 7805

A fixed linear regulator such as the 7805 controls its output by operating a pass element in its linear region. It is easy to understand and useful for modest current, but the voltage difference between input and output is converted into heat.

7805 TO-220 pinout and basic connection

Common 7805 TO-220 front-view pin order is IN, GND, OUT, with the text facing you and leads pointing down. Package variants and other regulator families differ; confirm the exact datasheet and tab connection before wiring or attaching a heatsink.

Practical 1 · Regulated 5 V rail

Use a current-limited DC source around 8–9 V for this bench activity, not an exposed mains-derived supply.

  • 7805 regulator, TO-220 package.
  • Input and output capacitors as recommended by the exact manufacturer's datasheet, for example 0.33 µF input and 0.1 µF output placed close to the pins.
  • Optional additional bulk capacitors if the wiring/source requires them.
  • 5 V LED test load with suitable series resistor, or a small 5 V rated resistive load.
  • Multimeter; current-limited bench supply.

Wiring: supply positive to IN, supply negative to GND, take +5 V from OUT. Connect both bypass capacitors between their respective input/output nodes and GND. Verify pin order before applying power.

Measure and observe

  1. Set the bench supply to 8 V with a conservative current limit before connecting the circuit.
  2. Measure input voltage directly at the regulator IN and GND pins.
  3. Measure output voltage at OUT and GND with no load.
  4. Connect the 5 V test load and measure again.
  5. Increase load only within the regulator, supply, wiring and thermal limits.
  6. After a short controlled run, disconnect power and check whether the package is becoming excessively hot. Do not touch a hot heatsink or package.

Record input, output and load current. Compare the output under no-load and loaded conditions.

Thermal reality: for a linear regulator, approximate pass-element dissipation is (VIN − VOUT) × ILOAD. At 9 V input, 5 V output and 0.25 A, this is about 1 W before small quiescent-current effects. That can make a small TO-220 package very hot without suitable thermal design.

04 · SWITCHING REGULATORS

Use a buck converter when efficiency matters

A buck converter reduces a higher DC input to a lower DC output by rapidly switching an electronic device. An inductor, capacitor and feedback controller transfer and store energy so the output is regulated. Unlike a linear regulator, it does not normally burn the full input-output voltage difference as heat.

Functional buck converter diagram

Simplified functional topology, not a complete controller-level schematic. Real buck converters include a controller, gate drive, switching device, diode or synchronous switch, inductor, capacitors, feedback and protection features.

Practical 2 · Adjustable buck module

  • Commercial adjustable DC-DC buck module with documented input/output range and current rating.
  • Current-limited DC bench supply within the module's specified input range.
  • Multimeter; suitable resistive load; optional thermometer.
  1. Disconnect the load. Set the source to a voltage inside the module's input specification.
  2. Power the module and measure its output.
  3. Adjust the trim control slowly while measuring output. Do not assume its factory setting is safe.
  4. Set the required output, switch off, connect the load, then power up and measure again.
  5. Observe output under increasing load without exceeding the module's ratings.

05 · REGULATION AND POWER QUALITY

What should you measure?

Line regulation

Change the input within the regulator's allowed operating range while holding the load constant. Observe how much the output changes. A regulated supply should limit this variation.

Load regulation

Keep the input constant and vary the load current. Measure the output at each load. Output droop can come from the regulator, source impedance, wiring, connectors or current limiting.

Ripple and noise

Use an oscilloscope with a suitable probe and grounding method to inspect ripple. A multimeter may show average DC but cannot fully characterise ripple or fast transients. Avoid unsafe ground connections.

Protection

Consider overcurrent, short-circuit, thermal shutdown, reverse polarity, input surge, output overvoltage and reverse current. Do not assume a cheap module provides every protection feature.

Never use an earth-grounded oscilloscope probe on an unknown or mains-referenced circuit. This module's measurements are confined to isolated extra-low-voltage supplies. If the source or reference is uncertain, stop and ask a qualified supervisor.

06 · CHOOSE A SUPPLY FOR A REAL CIRCUIT

Match the supply to the load

Estimate the load's normal and peak current, check start-up behaviour, and include margin without oversizing blindly. A microcontroller board, relay, motor, radio module and LED strip can have very different current profiles even when they share a nominal voltage.

Worked selection example

Suppose a 5 V control circuit draws 120 mA normally and a small sensor/radio burst raises the measured peak to 220 mA. The supply must maintain its output at that peak, tolerate start-up behaviour, and provide appropriate transient response. A 5 V, 500 mA regulated supply may be a reasonable candidate if its ratings, noise and protection suit the actual circuit. The rating is available capacity, not current forced into the load.

Practical 3 · Compare no-load and loaded output

  1. Measure the unloaded output of a regulated 5 V supply.
  2. Connect a known resistive load, calculate expected current using Ohm's law and verify resistor power.
  3. Measure the output again and calculate the change.
  4. Repeat with a different load within the supply rating.
  5. Record the supply temperature and look for abnormal heating, noise or instability.

Do not create a short circuit to test protection. Use an electronic load or a known resistor and stay within rated limits.

07 · TROUBLESHOOTING

Diagnose the power path

Output is too low

Check input voltage under load, polarity, rectifier orientation, regulator pinout, dropout/headroom, current limit and wiring resistance.

Excessive ripple

Check reservoir capacitance, capacitor polarity and condition, load current, rectifier frequency and connections. Measure ripple at the relevant node.

Regulator gets hot

Calculate dissipation, check load current and input voltage, inspect for output shorts, and assess package/heatsink thermal resistance and airflow.

Buck output unstable

Check that input/output are within range, load is suitable, wiring is short and secure, adjustment is stable, and the module has not entered current or thermal limiting.

Investigation tasks

  1. Measure the rectified voltage before and after adding a reservoir capacitor.
  2. Increase the load current and observe how ripple changes.
  3. Compare a 7805 and a buck module at the same input, output and load. Record temperature and output ripple.
  4. Estimate linear-regulator dissipation before connecting the load, then compare with observed heating.
  5. Explain why a 7805 cannot regulate correctly if its input falls too close to the required output voltage.
08 · SAFETY AND DESIGN LIMITS

Power supply work deserves respect

  • Use only certified isolated extra-low-voltage AC sources or current-limited DC bench supplies for these practicals.
  • Never connect exposed circuits directly to household mains. No transformerless/capacitive-dropper construction, open mains wiring or live-primary experiments.
  • Electrolytic capacitors are polarised. Check polarity and voltage rating; discharge stored energy safely before handling.
  • Rectifier diodes and regulators need adequate current, voltage and thermal ratings.
  • Do not exceed the safe operating area of a regulator, module, resistor, diode or load.
  • Never test short-circuit protection by deliberately shorting a supply output.
  • Use appropriate fusing, enclosure, strain relief and supervision for any later product design.

For component-specific limits, consult the datasheet of the exact 7805 or regulator part and the manufacturer's documentation for the selected buck module.
