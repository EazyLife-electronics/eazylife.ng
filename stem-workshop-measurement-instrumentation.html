Workshop Measurement and Instrumentation

Learn how to measure what a circuit is actually doing. Use a digital multimeter for voltage, current, resistance and continuity; understand oscilloscope waveforms; and use a signal generator as a controlled test source.

01 · MEASUREMENT IS PART OF ENGINEERING

A reading is only useful when the connection is correct

Measurement helps you compare a design with its expected behaviour, locate faults and make decisions from evidence. Before connecting an instrument, identify the quantity, the expected range, the correct terminals and whether the circuit must be powered.

What are you trying to know?

  • Voltage: electrical potential difference between two points.
  • Current: the rate of charge flow through a path.
  • Resistance: opposition to current in a component or path.
  • Continuity: whether a low-resistance path exists between two points.
  • Waveform: how voltage changes over time.
  • Frequency: how often a repeating event occurs each second.

Before every measurement

  1. Read the instrument and probe ratings.
  2. Inspect leads, insulation, probe tips and fuses.
  3. Choose the correct function and input socket.
  4. Start with a suitable range above the expected value if the meter is not auto-ranging.
  5. Connect the probes with the circuit in the required power state.
  6. Record the value, unit, range and test conditions.

Scope of this lesson: practical activities use isolated extra-low-voltage DC or low-voltage function-generator outputs. Household mains, live distribution boards, inverter internals and high-energy circuits are outside this beginner workshop. A normal grounded oscilloscope probe ground clip is usually connected to protective earth; never attach it to an arbitrary live node.

02 · DIGITAL MULTIMETER

Know the sockets, selector and measurement path

A digital multimeter (DMM) does not connect in the same way for every quantity. Voltage is measured across two points, current is measured in series with a load, and resistance/continuity is checked only on an unpowered circuit after stored energy has been safely discharged.

Typical DMM front panel and lead sockets

This is a generic teaching layout. Socket labels and current ranges differ between meters. Always follow the markings and manual for the exact instrument.

Voltage measurement · parallel

Place the probes across the component or two nodes. The circuit remains connected. Observe polarity for a DC reading; a negative sign means the probes are reversed relative to the meter's reference.

Current measurement · series

Open the circuit and insert the meter so the load current flows through it. Move the red lead to the correct fused current socket, select the correct current mode and begin on a suitable range.

Never place a meter in current mode directly across a battery or supply. That creates a near-short circuit through the meter and can blow its fuse, damage the instrument or cause injury. Return the red lead to the voltage socket immediately after current measurement.

03 · RESISTANCE, CONTINUITY AND DIODE TEST

Test components without powering the circuit

Resistance

Disconnect power, verify the circuit is de-energised and safely discharge capacitors. Isolate one component lead when parallel paths would affect the reading. The meter injects a small test signal and calculates resistance.

Continuity

The continuity beeper is a quick low-resistance path check. The threshold varies by meter. A beep does not prove that a connection is suitable for high current, and no beep does not always mean a component is faulty.

Diode-test mode

A DMM diode mode applies a small test current and displays a forward voltage for a conducting junction. A silicon signal diode may show roughly 0.5–0.8 V under the meter's test conditions, but the value depends on diode type and test current. Reverse the probes to check reverse blocking. Some LEDs may glow faintly; some meters do not provide enough voltage to test every LED.

Do not infer a diode's full current rating, reverse-voltage rating or switching speed from this simple test.

04 · RANGES, RESOLUTION AND UNCERTAINTY

A displayed number is not the whole measurement

Resolution is the smallest displayed increment for a selected range. Accuracy describes closeness to the true value within stated conditions. Precision describes repeatability. A stable display can still be inaccurate if the instrument is poorly calibrated or used outside its specified conditions.

Explore digital resolution

For this teaching calculator, resolution is estimated as range ÷ display counts. Real meters may use different count conventions, range endpoints and specifications.

Estimated display increment0.00333 V

A smaller range usually gives finer resolution, provided the reading remains within range.

What contributes to uncertainty?

  • Instrument accuracy specification and calibration state.
  • Resolution and display rounding.
  • Probe contact resistance and loose connections.
  • Temperature and component tolerance.
  • Meter loading, especially when measuring high-impedance circuits.
  • Human reading, setup and recording errors.

When comparing readings, keep the setup consistent and record the instrument range and conditions.

05 · OSCILLOSCOPE FUNDAMENTALS

See how voltage changes with time

An oscilloscope plots voltage vertically against time horizontally. The vertical scale is volts per division (V/div), and the horizontal scale is seconds per division (s/div). Triggering stabilises a repeating waveform so that its shape can be examined.

Oscilloscope screen and controls

Illustrative waveform display. The example grid and scales are teaching values, not measurements from a physical oscilloscope.

Read the waveform

  • Peak voltage: from centre reference to one peak.
  • Peak-to-peak: from the highest to lowest point.
  • Period (T): time for one complete cycle.
  • Frequency (f): 1 ÷ T.
  • Duty cycle: high-time divided by period × 100% for a two-state waveform.
  • Offset: waveform centre relative to the zero-volt reference.

Probe and ground connection

For a conventional earth-referenced bench oscilloscope, connect the probe ground clip only to the circuit's safe common/ground reference. Confirm the scope and circuit grounding arrangement before connecting. Use a properly rated differential probe or isolated measurement method when the measurement requires it.

Important: do not defeat the oscilloscope protective-earth connection or use an isolation transformer as a casual workaround. Never connect a grounded probe clip to a mains conductor, switching node or unknown potential. If you are not certain the circuit is safely referenced, stop and obtain qualified supervision.

Probe attenuation matters

A ×10 passive probe reduces the signal at the oscilloscope input by a factor of ten. The scope channel must be configured for the same attenuation, or the displayed voltage will be wrong by a factor of ten.

Check the probe switch, channel menu, compensation and bandwidth. A poorly compensated probe can distort square-wave edges. Probe capacitance and input impedance can also affect high-impedance circuits.

Do not assume: every scope automatically detects probe attenuation. Verify the setting on the actual instrument.

07 · SIGNAL GENERATOR

Create a known signal to test a circuit

A function or signal generator produces a controlled waveform such as a sine, square or triangle wave. You can set its frequency, amplitude and offset, then observe how a circuit responds. The displayed amplitude convention and output impedance vary by generator, so verify the manual and actual output.

Signal generator connected to a low-voltage test circuit

Conceptual low-voltage setup. Choose any series protection or coupling components based on the circuit under test; this drawing is not a universal generator wiring prescription.

Start with a conservative test

  1. Check the generator's output limits and load specification.
  2. Set a low amplitude, zero offset and a modest frequency.
  3. Connect the signal and reference to the intended input.
  4. Observe the input and output with a correctly grounded measurement setup.
  5. Increase one variable at a time and record the response.

Generator output is not always what you expect

Some generators specify amplitude for a 50 Ω load. If the output is connected to a high-impedance input, the measured amplitude may be approximately twice the value shown, depending on the generator design and configuration. Check whether the amplitude display assumes 50 Ω or high impedance.

Do not connect a generator output to a circuit that can drive voltage back into the generator unless the equipment is designed for it.

08 · PRACTICAL WORKSHOP

Three investigations using real instruments

Practical 1 · Verify a resistor network

Materials: isolated 5 V DC supply, 1 kΩ and 2.2 kΩ resistors, breadboard, DMM and jumper wires.

  1. With power disconnected, measure each resistor and record the actual values.
  2. Build a series pair and inspect for accidental shorts.
  3. Apply the 5 V supply. Measure voltage across the complete network and across each resistor.
  4. Calculate expected current using measured resistance values and compare it with the measured voltage drops.
  5. Power off before changing the circuit. Reconfigure the resistors in parallel and compare total resistance with the ideal calculation.

Record: nominal value, measured value, supply voltage, voltage drops and calculated current. Explain small differences using tolerance and instrument accuracy.

Practical 2 · Observe a low-voltage waveform

Materials: low-voltage function generator, oscilloscope with suitable probe and a simple resistive test load.

  1. Confirm the generator output and oscilloscope grounding arrangements before connecting.
  2. Set a small sine wave, for example 1 kHz and 1 V peak-to-peak, with zero offset, subject to the generator's specifications.
  3. Connect the signal to the test input and the scope probe tip to the signal node. Connect the ground clip only to the permitted common reference.
  4. Adjust volts/div and seconds/div until several cycles are visible.
  5. Measure peak-to-peak voltage and period from the grid; calculate frequency and compare it with the generator setting.
  6. Repeat with a square wave and observe the effect of probe compensation and bandwidth where the equipment permits.

Use only a low-voltage isolated signal source and a safe, earth-referenced circuit arrangement.

Practical 3 · Find an open connection

Materials: unpowered practice board with a prepared open link, DMM and circuit diagram.

  1. Disconnect the supply and verify stored energy is safely discharged.
  2. Use continuity mode to test the intended path from end to end.
  3. Divide the path into sections and identify where continuity is lost.
  4. Inspect the connector, solder joint or track at that location.
  5. Repair only if trained to do so, then repeat continuity and short checks.
  6. Reconnect power only after the board has been inspected and cleared.

Never use continuity mode on an energised circuit.

09 · TROUBLESHOOTING AND GOOD PRACTICE

When readings do not make sense

Meter shows OL or overload

The selected range may be too low, the circuit may be open, or the measured resistance may exceed the instrument range. Check the function, socket, range and circuit state before trying again.

Voltage reading is unexpectedly low

Check source voltage under load, probe contact, common reference, supply current limit, and whether the meter's input resistance is loading a high-impedance node.

Current reads zero

Check that the meter is truly in series, the red lead is in the correct current socket, the fuse is intact and the selected range is appropriate. Power down before changing connections.

Oscilloscope waveform is unstable

Check trigger source, trigger level, coupling, time base, probe ground, probe attenuation and signal amplitude. Confirm the input signal is actually periodic.

Scope voltage is ten times wrong

Check whether the probe is set to ×1 or ×10 and whether the channel menu uses the same factor. Check the generator's load setting too.

Continuity beeps through an unexpected path

Parallel components, protection diodes, coil windings and shared ground paths can provide alternate current paths. Isolate the component or inspect the schematic before declaring a short.

Good engineering record: write down the circuit revision, instrument model, probe/accessory, function, range, connection points, measured value, unit, supply conditions and date. A reading without its setup is difficult to reproduce.
