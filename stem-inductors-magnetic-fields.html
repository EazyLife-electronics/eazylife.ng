Inductors and Magnetic Fields

Build a coil, observe its magnetic field, then investigate how an inductor behaves in a real DC circuit.

01 · WHAT AN INDUCTOR DOES

A coil turns electric current into a magnetic field

When current flows through a wire, a magnetic field forms around it. Winding the wire into a coil makes the fields from neighbouring turns reinforce one another. An iron or ferrite core can concentrate the field. A coil designed to provide a useful amount of inductance is called an inductor.

Single straight conductor

The magnetic field forms circular paths around the conductor. The right-hand grip rule relates current direction to field direction.

Coil / solenoid

The fields reinforce through the middle of the coil. A magnetic core changes the strength and behaviour of the field.

Important distinction: inductance is not the same as winding resistance. A multimeter in resistance mode measures the copper wire's DC resistance, not the inductance in henries (H).

02 · THE PRINCIPLE

An inductor resists a change in current

When current through an inductor changes, a voltage is induced that opposes that change. This is described by:

vL = L × (di/dt)

L is inductance in henries, and di/dt is the rate at which current changes. The polarity depends on the reference directions chosen. This is the circuit form of Lenz's law.

  • At switch-on, current does not jump instantly to its final value in an ideal inductor.
  • With a steady DC supply after the transient, an ideal inductor behaves like a short circuit. A real coil still has winding resistance.
  • When current is switched off, the inductor tries to keep current flowing. If no safe path exists, the voltage can rise sharply.
Stored energy: E = ½ × L × I²

Energy is stored in the magnetic field. Doubling current stores four times as much energy, if inductance remains constant.

03 · PRACTICAL 1

Build a simple electromagnet

This first activity makes the magnetic field visible through its effect on a compass or small steel objects. It introduces the coil before moving to inductance and switching.

Materials

  • 1.5–3 m insulated copper wire, preferably enamelled magnet wire
  • One iron nail or soft-iron bolt (optional core)
  • 5 V DC supply or 3 × AA battery holder
  • 100 Ω, 0.5 W resistor (current limiter)
  • Momentary push-button or switch
  • Small compass and a few paper clips
  • Sandpaper for removing enamel at wire ends, if needed

Connection diagram

The resistor limits current. Press the switch only briefly during the investigation. Do not connect the coil directly across a battery without current limiting.

  1. Wind 30–60 turns of insulated wire around the nail. Keep the turns close together and leave two free leads.
  2. If using enamelled wire, carefully remove enamel from the final 10–15 mm of each lead. Check for clean copper before connecting.
  3. Wire the supply positive to the 100 Ω resistor, then to one coil lead. Connect the other coil lead to the push switch, then to supply negative.
  4. Place the compass beside the coil. Briefly press the switch and observe the compass needle. Release the switch and note what changes.
  5. Bring a few paper clips near the nail while the switch is pressed briefly. Compare the attraction with and without the iron core.

Safety: A coil can heat up. Use the resistor, short activation periods and a low-voltage supply. Disconnect between trials. Stop if the wire, resistor or battery becomes warm. Do not use a mains adapter with unknown output or exposed terminals.

Record your observations

Does the compass needle move when current flows? Does its direction change if you reverse the supply connections? Does adding the iron core change the paper-clip attraction? Explain your observations using current direction and magnetic field.

04 · PRACTICAL 2

Investigate current in a series RL circuit

Now use a ready-made inductor to investigate the transient. The series resistor limits current and also gives you a safe way to estimate current by measuring its voltage drop.

Protected RL test circuit

The diode is connected across the complete series resistor-and-inductor load, with its cathode at the positive supply side. It provides a recirculation path when the switch opens. The diagram is a functional circuit schematic; follow the connection nodes, not the drawing scale.

Components

  • 5 V isolated DC supply
  • 100 Ω, 0.5 W resistor
  • 100 mH to 1 H inductor, current rating at least 50 mA
  • 1N400x diode (or suitable general-purpose rectifier diode)
  • SPST switch or push-button
  • Digital multimeter; oscilloscope recommended for observing the transient
  • Breadboard and jumper wires

Build and measure

  1. With power disconnected, wire the supply, resistor, inductor and switch in series.
  2. Connect the diode across the complete resistor-plus-inductor path. Its cathode (banded end) goes to +5 V; its anode goes to the switched low side.
  3. Connect the voltmeter across the 100 Ω resistor. Use DC voltage mode, not current mode.
  4. Check for shorts and confirm diode polarity before applying power.
  5. Close the switch. Measure the resistor voltage. Estimate current using I = VR/100 Ω.
  6. Open the switch and observe the voltage decay. An oscilloscope across the resistor makes the transient easier to see.

Example: If the resistor drop is 2.0 V, current is 2.0/100 = 0.020 A = 20 mA. The resistor is in series, so its current is the same as the inductor current.

Expected observations: current rises after the switch closes rather than jumping instantly in the ideal model. After a long time, it approaches the value set mainly by supply voltage and total series resistance. When the switch opens, current continues briefly through the diode and load, then decays. The diode limits the voltage that would otherwise rise across the load.

Never remove the flyback diode for a casual demonstration. Without a suitable clamp path, the switching voltage can damage a switch, instrument or circuit and may create a shock or arcing hazard. Do not use mains, automotive ignition coils or high-energy inductors in this activity.

06 · UNDERSTANDING THE TIME CONSTANT

Why does L/R matter?

For a simple series RL circuit, the time constant is:

τ = L/R

With inductance in henries and resistance in ohms, the time constant is in seconds. After one time constant, current has reached about 63.2% of its final value. After five time constants, it is above 99% of the final value.

Increase inductance

With resistance unchanged, a larger inductance makes current change more slowly.

Increase resistance

With inductance unchanged, a larger resistance reduces the time constant and also reduces final current for a fixed supply.

07 · REAL INDUCTORS

Types, ratings and applications

Air-core

No magnetic core saturation, often used in RF and tuned circuits.

Ferrite-core

Common in high-frequency switching supplies and filters.

Iron-core

Used in many lower-frequency power and magnetic applications.

Before selecting an inductor, check inductance and tolerance, saturation current, RMS current and temperature rise, DC resistance, core material, operating frequency and insulation. Saturation can reduce inductance and cause current to rise unexpectedly.

Inductors are used in filters, chokes, buck and boost converters, relays, solenoids, motors and transformers (which use magnetic coupling between windings).

08 · COMMON MISTAKES

Check these before troubleshooting

  • Assuming an inductor blocks DC permanently. In steady state, current can flow through its winding resistance.
  • Assuming an ohmmeter reading tells you the inductance value.
  • Disconnecting a coil under current without a suitable flyback or clamp path.
  • Choosing an inductor using only its inductance value while ignoring saturation and current ratings.
  • Connecting an electromagnet directly to a battery without a current-limiting resistor.
  • Assuming a flyback diode can be placed in either direction. A reverse-biased diode is required during normal energised operation in the simple DC coil circuit.
