01 · THE ELECTRONICS WORKBENCH

Good workshop habits produce better circuits

Workshop practice is not just about owning tools. It is a repeatable process: plan the circuit, identify components, prepare the work area, assemble carefully, inspect, test and document. A neat build is easier to troubleshoot and safer to maintain.

Basic hand tools

  • Small side cutters for trimming component leads.
  • Needle-nose pliers for shaping leads and handling small parts.
  • Wire strippers sized for the conductor being used.
  • Small screwdrivers, tweezers and a component organiser.
  • Helping hands or a PCB holder to keep the work steady.
  • Eye protection when cutting leads or wire.

Electrical test equipment

  • Digital multimeter with suitable voltage, resistance and continuity ranges.
  • Current-limited extra-low-voltage bench supply for controlled tests.
  • Breadboard and jumper wires for temporary prototypes.
  • Magnifier or inspection light for solder joints and PCB tracks.
  • Optional: oscilloscope, logic analyser and LCR meter for more advanced investigations.

Set up a safe, organised bench

Conceptual workbench layout. Keep the hot iron in its stand, route leads away from the hot tip, and position fume extraction so solder fumes move away from your breathing zone.

02 · CHOOSING A BUILD METHOD

Breadboard, perfboard or printed circuit board?

Solderless breadboard

Reusable temporary prototyping. No soldering required. Internal clips connect groups of holes in a defined pattern. Check the board's actual rail and row layout; not every board has continuous power rails.

Perfboard / stripboard

Useful for semi-permanent prototypes and small one-off circuits. Perfboard usually has isolated pads; stripboard has copper strips that must be cut where connections should stop. Plan the underside before soldering.

Custom printed circuit board (PCB)

A designed board with defined copper layers, tracks, pads, vias and component footprints. A PCB layout must match the schematic electrically and the real components mechanically.

03 · THROUGH-HOLE SOLDERING

Make a sound electrical and mechanical joint

Soldering joins clean metal surfaces using a heated solder alloy. The iron heats both the component lead and the copper pad; solder then wets both surfaces. A good joint is formed by heat transfer and wetting, not by piling solder onto a cold connection.

What happens at the joint?

Simplified cross-section. The solder should wet the pad and lead, forming a smooth concave fillet. The actual appearance depends on solder alloy, flux, pad finish and process.

Equipment and consumables

  • Temperature-controlled soldering iron with a suitable clean tip.
  • Stable iron stand and tip-cleaning brass wool or a damp sponge used as specified by the station.
  • Electronics-grade solder wire with suitable flux core.
  • Separate electronics-grade flux when needed; never use plumbing acid flux on electronics.
  • Desoldering braid or a suitable solder pump.
  • PCB holder, side cutters and eye protection.

Temperature and solder choice

There is no single correct iron temperature for every joint. Tip size, thermal mass, board copper, solder alloy and component sensitivity all matter. Use the solder and station manufacturer's guidance, a suitable tip and the lowest practical temperature that makes a quick, complete joint. Lead-free alloys often need different process settings from tin-lead alloys.

Do not use soldering as a reason to inhale flux fumes. Use effective local fume extraction and follow the product safety data sheet.

Step-by-step through-hole soldering

  1. Identify and place. Confirm component value, polarity, pinout and board reference before insertion. Check the component datasheet when orientation is uncertain.
  2. Secure the board. Place the PCB in a holder. Keep the iron in its stand until ready to solder.
  3. Prepare the tip. Use a clean, lightly tinned tip. A small amount of solder on the tip improves heat transfer, but the joint itself must be heated at the pad and lead.
  4. Heat both surfaces. Touch the tip so it contacts the copper pad and component lead at the same time.
  5. Feed solder to the joint. Feed solder to the heated pad/lead junction, not merely onto the iron tip. Allow enough solder to wet the surfaces without flooding the pad.
  6. Remove solder, then iron. Withdraw the solder wire first, then lift the iron smoothly. Avoid moving the component while the joint solidifies.
  7. Inspect and trim. After the joint has cooled, inspect it under good light and trim the lead without stressing the joint. Collect trimmed leads safely.
  8. Clean if required. Follow the flux manufacturer's guidance and the board/component requirements. Some no-clean fluxes are designed to remain; other residues should be removed using an appropriate process.

Hot-tool safety: the iron tip can cause severe burns and ignite nearby material. Keep it in its stand, never leave it unattended while powered, do not catch a falling iron, wash hands after handling solder, and use eye protection when trimming leads. Keep children and loose cables away from the hot work area.

04 · JOINT INSPECTION

Recognise good joints and common defects

Well-formed joint

A good joint has wetted the pad and lead, with a stable fillet and no unintended bridge to adjacent pads. Appearance alone is not proof of electrical reliability; test the circuit too.

Insufficient wetting / disturbed joint

Insufficient heat, contamination, movement during cooling or poor flux action can cause incomplete wetting. Rework only after the joint is safely reheated using an appropriate technique.

Solder bridge

Excess solder can connect pads that should remain electrically separate. Remove the bridge with suitable desoldering braid or a solder pump, then inspect and test before power is applied.

Cold-looking / cracked / lifted pad

A dull or irregular surface can be a warning sign, but solder alloy and flux affect appearance. A cracked joint may move under vibration. A lifted pad or damaged track can break the copper connection from the board.

Do not pull hard on a component lead. Use the right desoldering method and inspect both sides of the board.

05 · MULTIMETER TESTING AND FAULT-FINDING

Inspect first, then test without power

Continuity test

With the circuit disconnected from all power sources, use continuity or resistance mode to check that intended connections are present and unwanted shorts are absent. Capacitors can affect readings while charging from the meter.

Safe test sequence

  1. Disconnect power and external connections.
  2. Inspect component orientation, solder bridges, loose strands and damaged tracks.
  3. Check continuity along intended nets.
  4. Check for unintended shorts between supply rails and ground.
  5. Verify resistance values and diode polarity where appropriate.
  6. Only then power from a current-limited extra-low-voltage source, starting with a conservative current limit.
  7. Measure expected voltages and compare them with the schematic.

Important: never use resistance or continuity mode on an energised circuit. Discharge capacitors safely and verify the circuit is unpowered before testing. Follow your multimeter's manual for input limits and probe connections.

06 · PCB FUNDAMENTALS

From schematic to physical copper

A schematic expresses electrical relationships. A PCB layout assigns real component footprints and copper paths to implement those relationships. The board must be electrically correct, physically manufacturable and suitable for its operating environment.

Typical PCB design workflow

  1. Draw and review the schematic.
  2. Assign correct footprints using datasheets and physical measurements.
  3. Define board outline, mounting holes and connector locations.
  4. Place components with signal flow, serviceability and heat in mind.
  5. Route tracks, add planes and vias where appropriate.
  6. Run electrical-rule and design-rule checks.
  7. Inspect the 2D/3D board against the real enclosure and parts.
  8. Generate manufacturing outputs and review them before ordering.

PCB vocabulary

  • Footprint: pad geometry and markings for a specific package.
  • Track/trace: copper path connecting pads.
  • Via: plated connection between copper layers.
  • Pad: conductive area for a component lead or terminal.
  • Solder mask: insulating coating that leaves pads exposed.
  • Silkscreen: printed reference labels and board markings.
  • Copper pour/plane: a larger copper region, often used for ground or power distribution.
  • Gerber and drill files: common manufacturing outputs, subject to the fabricator's requirements.

Example: schematic connection versus PCB routing

Illustrative comparison only, not a production design. Real footprints, pad sizes, trace widths, clearances, copper layers and connector pin numbering must be checked.

Layout decisions that matter

  • Use the exact package and pin numbering from the component datasheet.
  • Place decoupling capacitors close to the IC power pins with a short return path.
  • Keep high-current paths appropriately wide and short, based on copper thickness, temperature rise and applicable design guidance.
  • Keep sensitive analog signals away from noisy switching and motor-current paths where possible.
  • Respect creepage, clearance, isolation and enclosure requirements for the actual voltage category.
  • Provide mechanical support for connectors, switches and components subject to force or vibration.

Design checks before fabrication

  • ERC: electrical-rule check of the schematic.
  • DRC: design-rule check of the PCB layout.
  • Footprint and pin mapping review against datasheets.
  • Track width, current, voltage drop and temperature review.
  • Minimum track/space, drill and annular-ring checks against the fabricator's capability.
  • Board outline, mounting, connector orientation and silkscreen review.
  • Gerber/drill viewer inspection before ordering.

PCB safety boundary: this lesson focuses on extra-low-voltage educational circuits. Do not design or fabricate mains-connected, high-voltage, high-energy battery or safety-critical boards from these beginner guidelines. Those require standards-based engineering review, isolation design, creepage/clearance analysis, protection coordination and qualified testing.

07 · PRACTICAL ACTIVITIES

Build, solder, inspect and verify

Practical 1 · Solder a simple LED test board

Materials: low-voltage isolated supply (3–5 V), LED, suitable series resistor, small perfboard, hookup wire, soldering station, electronics solder, fume extraction and multimeter.

  1. Calculate a safe LED series resistor using the supply voltage, LED forward voltage and target current. Choose a standard resistor with adequate power rating.
  2. Draw the simple circuit and identify the LED anode and cathode before placing components.
  3. Arrange the resistor and LED on perfboard. Keep the connections short and leave enough spacing to inspect pads.
  4. Secure the board and solder one lead at a time using the method in this lesson.
  5. Inspect every joint under good light. Look for bridges, incomplete wetting and disturbed joints.
  6. With power disconnected, use continuity mode to verify the intended path and check for unintended rail-to-rail shorts.
  7. Connect the current-limited low-voltage supply with the correct polarity and a conservative current limit. Measure the voltage across the resistor and calculate current using I = V/R.

Record: resistor value, measured resistor voltage, calculated LED current and any difference from the target.

Practical 2 · Rework and fault diagnosis

Materials: a spare low-voltage practice board with a deliberately prepared open connection or solder bridge, magnifier, solder wick or pump and multimeter.

  1. Keep the board unpowered and inspect the circuit visually.
  2. Use the schematic to identify the intended connection and the fault symptom.
  3. Check continuity to locate an open path, or inspect adjacent pads for an unintended bridge.
  4. Use suitable desoldering braid or a pump to remove excess solder without overheating the pad.
  5. Re-form the joint and allow it to cool without movement.
  6. Repeat continuity and short checks before applying power.
  7. Document the original fault, the correction and the verification result.

Do not create a fault by shorting a live supply. Use a prepared unpowered practice board.

Practical 3 · Turn a schematic into a simple PCB layout

Materials: PCB design software, a simple low-voltage LED/resistor schematic, component datasheets and a board fabrication design-rule template.

  1. Draw a supply connector, resistor and LED in the schematic and label the nets.
  2. Assign footprints that match the actual through-hole component lead spacing and connector pitch.
  3. Place the parts on a small board outline, considering polarity markings and access to the connector.
  4. Route the positive path through the resistor and LED to the return. Confirm the net connections.
  5. Run ERC and DRC, then inspect the board in 2D and 3D.
  6. Review the footprint, polarity, silkscreen and manufacturing constraints before exporting any fabrication files.

This is a design exercise. Fabrication is optional; verify all outputs and the manufacturer's rules before ordering.

08 · WORKSHOP TROUBLESHOOTING

Common problems and how to respond

Solder will not flow onto the pad

Check tip cleanliness, tip contact, heat transfer, oxidation, surface contamination and flux compatibility. Do not compensate by holding the iron on the pad for a long time; excessive heat can lift pads and damage components.

Component moves while solder cools

Secure the board and component before soldering. Allow the joint to solidify without movement. Reinspect and rework a disturbed joint safely if needed.

Continuity beeps where it should not

Check for solder bridges, copper links on stripboard, shared ground paths, parallel components and capacitor charging effects. Use the schematic and resistance reading rather than relying only on the beeper.

PCB footprint does not fit

Check package variant, pin pitch, pad geometry, component lead diameter, orientation and manufacturer drawing. Do not stretch leads or force a component into an incorrect footprint.
