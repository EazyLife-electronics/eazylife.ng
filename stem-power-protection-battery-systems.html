Power Supply Protection and Battery Systems

Learn how to protect a power path, understand battery ratings and configurations, estimate usable energy and runtime, and test low-voltage systems responsibly.

01 · WHY PROTECTION MATTERS

A working supply also needs protection

A power source can deliver enough energy to damage wires, components or itself when something goes wrong. Protection is designed around the source, wiring, load, environment and likely faults. A fuse, regulator or battery-management system does not make an otherwise unsafe design automatically safe.

A protected DC power path

Functional block diagram only. Protection components must be selected and placed for the actual source, fault current, conductor size, load and device specifications.

Overcurrent and short-circuit protection

A fuse or circuit breaker interrupts a circuit when current and time exceed its operating characteristic. It is primarily there to protect wiring and equipment against fault energy. Its voltage rating, interrupting capacity, time-current curve and current rating all matter.

A fuse is normally placed close to the energy source so the unprotected length of wire is minimised. The fuse must be coordinated with the wire and the load, not chosen from load current alone.

Other protection functions

  • Reverse polarity: prevents damage when supply connections are reversed.
  • Overvoltage: limits or disconnects a voltage that exceeds the design range.
  • Undervoltage: disconnects or warns before a source is discharged too far.
  • Thermal protection: responds to excessive component or battery temperature.
  • Surge/transient protection: handles specified short-duration voltage events.

03 · BATTERY CHEMISTRY AND RATINGS

Voltage, capacity and energy are different quantities

A battery is an electrochemical source. Its voltage changes with chemistry, state of charge, temperature, load and age. Capacity describes charge delivered under stated test conditions; energy describes how much electrical work is available over time.

Voltage · V

Electrical potential. A cell's nominal voltage is a useful label, not a fixed voltage at every moment.

Capacity · Ah

Ampere-hours describe charge capacity under a specified discharge rate, temperature and cutoff voltage.

Energy · Wh

Watt-hours estimate stored energy. For a rough nominal estimate, Wh ≈ V × Ah.

Common rechargeable cell families

What a battery label does not tell you

A capacity number by itself does not guarantee the same runtime in every device. Discharge current, temperature, ageing, cutoff voltage, cell condition and conversion losses all affect usable energy. High current can reduce the capacity available from some chemistries.

Battery management system (BMS)

A suitable BMS may monitor cell-group voltage and temperature, disconnect for overcharge, over-discharge or overcurrent, and balance series-connected groups. Features vary widely. A BMS is not a substitute for a correctly designed charger, fuse, enclosure or thermal design.

04 · SERIES AND PARALLEL CONFIGURATIONS

Know what changes when cells are connected

Cells in series · voltage adds

For matched cells in series, nominal voltage adds. The amp-hour capacity remains approximately that of one cell/group, and every series group must stay within its permitted voltage range.

Cells in parallel · capacity adds

For compatible matched cells in parallel, voltage stays approximately the same while capacity and current capability may increase. Parallel cell connection requires careful matching and protection; do not experiment by paralleling loose lithium cells.

Series-parallel packs: a configuration such as 3S2P means three cell groups in series, with two matched cells in parallel in each group. Pack design also requires cell matching, interconnect design, monitoring, fusing and a compatible BMS/charger.

06 · CHARGING AND SAFE BATTERY USE

Charging is chemistry-specific

Use the correct charger

Different chemistries need different charging methods. Common lithium-ion cells use controlled constant-current/constant-voltage charging with a chemistry- and cell-specific voltage limit. NiMH and lead-acid use different charge termination or control methods. A generic voltage source is not a safe universal charger.

Respect cell limits

Observe maximum charge voltage, minimum discharge voltage, continuous and pulse current limits, temperature range and storage guidance. Do not rely on nominal voltage alone to determine whether a cell is full or empty.

Inspect before use

Do not use swollen, leaking, dented, punctured, corroded, overheated or unusually hot cells. Keep terminals protected from metal objects and prevent accidental short circuits.

Storage and disposal

Follow the manufacturer's storage state-of-charge and temperature guidance. Recycle batteries through suitable collection channels; do not burn, crush or place rechargeable cells in ordinary fires.

For this lesson, do not assemble or charge a lithium-ion pack from loose cells. Use an intact, commercially protected battery pack with its specified charger, or use AA cells in a suitable holder for the supervised low-voltage series demonstration. Never mix cell chemistry, model, age, capacity or state of charge.

07 · PRACTICAL INVESTIGATIONS

Measure a real low-voltage system

Practical 1 · Series-cell voltage

Materials: two identical AA cells of the same chemistry and condition, a suitable two-cell holder, multimeter and a small rated LED test circuit with series resistor.

  1. With the cells outside the holder, inspect them and confirm matching type and condition.
  2. Place both cells in the holder, observing the holder's polarity markings. Do not force a cell.
  3. Set the meter to DC volts and measure across the holder output.
  4. Compare the measured voltage with the sum of the individual cell voltages.
  5. Connect a suitable low-current test load and measure the output again.
  6. Disconnect the load and record the voltage change.

Observe: series connection increases voltage. It does not double the amp-hour capacity of the string.

Practical 2 · Fuse and load investigation

Materials: current-limited low-voltage bench supply, correctly rated fuse holder and fuse, known resistor load, multimeter and connecting leads.

  1. Calculate the expected load current from the selected supply voltage and resistor value.
  2. Check the resistor's power dissipation and choose a suitably rated part.
  3. With power off, place the fuse in series with the positive supply lead.
  4. Set a conservative current limit before energising the circuit.
  5. Measure the voltage across the load and the current through the circuit using the correct meter method.
  6. Switch off before changing any wiring. Never deliberately short the output to make the fuse operate.

Observe: the fuse is in series with the load path. Its actual protection behaviour depends on its time-current curve and fault conditions.

Practical 3 · Inspect a commercial USB power bank

Materials: an intact commercial power bank, compatible USB power meter and a modest USB load within the bank's rating.

  1. Read the power bank's input/output label and identify the rated voltage and current for the selected port.
  2. Connect the USB meter according to its instructions, then connect a compatible load.
  3. Observe output voltage and current while the load operates.
  4. Calculate output power using P = V × I.
  5. Compare the output rating with the battery's internal nominal voltage and explain why a conversion stage is needed.

Do not open the power bank or probe its internal battery. Stop if it becomes unusually hot, swells, smells abnormal or behaves unexpectedly.

08 · TROUBLESHOOTING AND DESIGN CHOICES

Think about the whole power path

Load resets when a motor starts

Check supply current capability, motor start/stall current, wiring resistance, connector rating, battery condition and regulator transient response. Consider separate motor and logic supplies with a deliberate grounding strategy.

Fuse opens during normal operation

Check actual steady and inrush current, fuse time-current characteristic, load faults, wiring and temperature. Do not simply install a larger fuse without checking conductor and equipment ratings.

Battery runtime is shorter than expected

Check average and peak load, conversion efficiency, cutoff voltage, battery age, temperature, discharge rate and whether the advertised capacity is specified at a different test condition.

Pack voltage looks correct but device fails

Measure voltage under load, verify connector polarity, check transient drops and confirm the device's input range. An unloaded voltage reading does not prove the supply can deliver the required current.

Design review checklist

  • What is the source's normal voltage, maximum voltage and available fault current?
  • What are the load's continuous, peak and start-up current requirements?
  • Are conductors, connectors, switches and protection devices correctly rated?
  • What happens under reverse polarity, overload, short circuit, overheating and low battery?
  • Is the charger compatible with the exact battery chemistry and series configuration?
  • Are enclosure, ventilation, insulation, strain relief and maintenance requirements addressed?
09 · SAFETY AND LIMITS

Battery energy must be handled responsibly

  • Use only intact, protected commercial battery packs and their specified chargers. For simple cell demonstrations, use matching AA cells in a suitable holder.
  • Do not build, charge, repair or modify lithium-ion packs as part of this beginner activity. Do not parallel loose lithium cells.
  • Never short battery terminals, puncture, crush, heat, solder directly to or disassemble cells.
  • Keep cells away from conductive loose objects and protect exposed terminals during storage and transport.
  • Do not mix chemistries, models, capacities, ages or states of charge in one pack.
  • Use a fuse close to the source where the design requires it, and coordinate the fuse with wiring, load and source fault current.
  • Disconnect damaged or abnormal batteries from service when safe to do so and follow local battery collection guidance.

These activities are educational low-voltage investigations. A real battery product requires a complete electrical, mechanical, thermal and regulatory safety review.
